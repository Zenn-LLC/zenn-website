Fifteen minutes each is enough to think, but not enough to think about everything. These habits win more games at this speed.

Before every move

Ask three questions, in this order:

  1. What did their last move do? Look for new attacks, threats to take something, and checks.
  2. Is anything of mine hanging? A piece that is attacked and not defended, or attacked more times than it is defended.
  3. Can I give check, take something, or attack something? Look at these forcing moves first. They are where most games are won.

Most games at club level are lost to a piece left hanging, not to deep strategy. This check takes a few seconds and saves more games than any opening.

Openings

  • Control the centre with a pawn or two, then bring out knights and bishops before moving the same piece twice.
  • Castle early. A king in the middle of the board is a target.
  • Don't bring the queen out early to chase pawns. It gets chased back and you lose time.
  • Pick one opening as White and one answer each to 1.e4 and 1.d4, and learn them well rather than many badly.

Use your clock

  • Spend time in the middle of the game, where the hard decisions are. Play the opening you know quickly.
  • When your opponent's clock is low, keep the position complicated and make them find moves.
  • When yours is low, play simple, safe moves. Pre-moves help for obvious recaptures, but check they can't be punished.
  • On Zentra a drawn position is won by whoever has more time left. If you're heading for a draw, being ahead on the clock wins.

Finishing won games

  • When you're ahead in material, trade pieces, not pawns. Simple positions are easier to win.
  • Watch for stalemate when your opponent has only a king left. Leave the king a square to move to until you can checkmate. On Zentra a stalemate is decided by the clock, so it may still go against you.
  • Learn the basic checkmates: king and queen, king and rook. They come up more than you'd think.

Learn from your games

After each game, open its review. Look at your mistakes and blunders first: each shows the better move. Patterns you keep missing are the ones to practise.
